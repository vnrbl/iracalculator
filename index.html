FAQ

Common IRA & beneficiary questions

Short answers on required minimum distributions, penalties, and planning.

Enacted under the SECURE Act and clarified by SECURE Act 2.0, the 10-year rule requires most non-spouse designated beneficiaries who inherit a traditional or Roth IRA after December 31, 2019, to fully distribute all assets by December 31 of the 10th year following the original owner's death.
It depends on whether the original owner reached their Required Beginning Date (RBD, age 73). Under the IRS final regulations (TD 10001, July 2024):
  • Owner died before RBD: annual distributions in years 1–9 are optional. The entire balance must be zero by December 31 of Year 10.
  • Owner died on or after RBD: you are legally required to take annual RMDs in years 1 through 9 based on your single life expectancy, and liquidate all remaining assets in Year 10.
Under SECURE Act 2.0, the excise penalty for failing to take a required minimum distribution is 25% of the shortfall amount, reduced to 10% if corrected in a timely manner via IRS Form 5329. Transitional penalty relief covered 2021–2024, but full enforcement applies going forward.
Yes — inherited Roth IRAs are subject to the 10-year rule for non-spouse beneficiaries. However, because qualified Roth distributions are tax-free and not subject to annual RMDs, beneficiaries generally do best by leaving the funds invested until Year 10.
Traditional IRA distributions are ordinary taxable income. Leaving a $250,000+ account to compound for a decade can push a single-year distribution into the 32%, 35%, or 37% federal brackets. Smoothing spreads withdrawals across years 1–10 to absorb room in the 12% and 22% brackets, often saving tens of thousands of dollars in total tax.

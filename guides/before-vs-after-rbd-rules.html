Death Before vs. After Required Beginning Date (RBD)

IRS Final Regulations 6 min read Treasury Decision 10001

When navigating an inherited traditional IRA under the 10-year rule, no single question creates more legal confusion than: "Did the account owner die before or after their Required Beginning Date (RBD)?"

The answer to this question completely changes your legal responsibilities under IRS rules. It determines whether you can legally choose to take zero distributions during Years 1 through 9, or whether failing to take an annual distribution triggers an IRS penalty.

What is the Required Beginning Date (RBD)?

The Required Beginning Date is the statutory deadline by which an IRA owner must begin taking annual Required Minimum Distributions from their pre-tax retirement accounts. Under the SECURE 2.0 Act, the RBD is defined as April 1 of the year following the calendar year in which the individual reaches their designated RMD age:

  • Age 70½: For individuals born before July 1, 1949.
  • Age 72: For individuals born between July 1, 1949, and December 31, 1950.
  • Age 73: For individuals born between January 1, 1951, and December 31, 1959.
  • Age 75: For individuals born on or after January 1, 1960.

Scenario A: Owner Died BEFORE Their Required Beginning Date

If the owner passed away prior to reaching their RBD (e.g., at age 68), the IRS applies the standard 10-Year Rule without annual minimum distribution mandates:

  • Years 1 through 9: You are not legally required to withdraw a single penny. You may withdraw as much or as little as you desire.
  • Year 10: The entire balance must be distributed by December 31 of the 10th anniversary year.
Strategic Consideration: Even though the IRS does not mandate annual withdrawals if the owner died before RBD, waiting until Year 10 will concentrate all taxes into a single taxable year, potentially creating an enormous tax spike.

Scenario B: Owner Died ON OR AFTER Their Required Beginning Date

If the owner died after their RBD (e.g., at age 76), the longstanding "At Least As Rapidly" (ALAR) rule applies. Because the deceased had already entered the distribution phase, the IRS requires that distributions continue at least as rapidly as under the owner's lifetime schedule.

Under the July 2024 final regulations (TD 10001), the rules are clear:

  1. Years 1 through 9: The beneficiary must take an annual RMD. The RMD is calculated using the beneficiary's single life expectancy factor from IRS Table I (Publication 590-B) in Year 1, reduced by 1.0 for each subsequent year.
  2. Year 10: The remaining balance must be completely liquidated by December 31 of the 10th year.

What About the Year of Death RMD?

If the deceased passed away on or after their RBD and had not yet completed their required distribution for the calendar year of death, that distribution must still be satisfied by December 31 of the year of death.

The beneficiary must take the year-of-death RMD (calculated using the deceased owner's Uniform Lifetime Table factor) and report it as taxable income on the beneficiary's tax return.

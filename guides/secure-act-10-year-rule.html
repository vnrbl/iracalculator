The Complete Guide to the SECURE Act 10-Year Rule

Updated for 2026 Tax Year 8 min read IRS TD 10001 Analysis

Before the passage of the Setting Every Community Up for Retirement Enhancement (SECURE) Act of 2019, beneficiaries of inherited traditional IRAs could take advantage of the venerable "Stretch IRA." By taking annual Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs) based strictly on their own individual life expectancy, an adult child in their 30s or 40s could stretch distributions—and tax deferral—over four or five decades.

The SECURE Act abolished the Stretch IRA for the vast majority of non-spouse beneficiaries, replacing it with a rigid 10-Year Liquidation Rule. In July 2024, the Department of the Treasury and the Internal Revenue Service released final regulations (Treasury Decision 10001), settling years of debate regarding whether beneficiaries must take annual distributions during years 1 through 9.

Key Rule Summary: The inherited account balance must be reduced to exactly zero dollars by December 31 of the tenth calendar year following the year of the account owner’s death. For example, if the original owner died on October 12, 2024, Year 1 begins on January 1, 2025, and the account must be entirely liquidated by December 31, 2034.

Who is Subject to the 10-Year Rule?

The 10-year rule applies specifically to Non-Eligible Designated Beneficiaries (NEDBs) inheriting traditional IRAs, Roth IRAs, 401(k) plans, 403(b)s, and 457(b) plans where the original owner passed away after December 31, 2019.

Common examples of Non-Eligible Designated Beneficiaries include:

  • Adult children who are neither chronically ill nor disabled.
  • Grandchildren, nieces, and nephews.
  • Friends, domestic partners, and non-spouse family members.
  • Siblings who are more than 10 years younger than the deceased account owner.

The Crucial Distinction: Did the Owner Reach Their RBD?

One of the most misunderstood aspects of the 10-year rule is the "At Least As Rapidly" rule. Whether you are legally obligated to take distributions in Years 1 through 9 depends entirely on the owner's Required Beginning Date (RBD):

1. Owner Died Before Their Required Beginning Date

If the decedent died before reaching age 73 (the current SECURE Act 2.0 RBD threshold), the beneficiary has maximum flexibility. The IRS does not require any distributions in Years 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, or 9. However, the entire account must be completely cleared by the end of Year 10. While leaving money invested seems attractive, it frequently leads to the dreaded "Year 10 Tax Bomb" discussed below.

2. Owner Died On or After Their Required Beginning Date

If the decedent had already started taking their own RMDs (or had reached age 73), the beneficiary must continue taking annual RMDs in Years 1 through 9. These annual distributions are calculated using the beneficiary’s Single Life Expectancy factor from IRS Publication 590-B. In Year 10, whatever balance remains must be liquidated in full.

Penalties for Non-Compliance (SECURE 2.0 Updates)

Failing to withdraw the required amount triggers an excise tax penalty under IRS Section 4974. Historically, this penalty was an exorbitant 50% of the shortfall. Under the SECURE 2.0 Act, this penalty has been reduced to:

  • 25% standard excise penalty on the shortfall.
  • 10% reduced penalty if the error is corrected in a timely manner (by filing Form 5329 and withdrawing the required funds within the two-year correction window).
Important Transition Note: Due to widespread confusion following the 2019 legislation, the IRS issued temporary waivers (Notices 2022-53, 2023-54, and 2024-35) waiving penalties for missed annual RMDs for tax years 2021 through 2024. However, with the publication of final regulations, full enforcement is mandatory for tax years 2025 and 2026 onwards.

How to Model Your 10-Year Distribution Strategy

Rather than taking minimum distributions or waiting until the final year, sophisticated beneficiaries use tax-bracket smoothing. By spreading withdrawals evenly or targeting specific federal tax ceilings (such as the 22% or 24% tax brackets), you can keep marginal rates low and preserve significant wealth.
